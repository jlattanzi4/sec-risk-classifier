How much of a risk disclosure is boilerplate?

Every public company lists the risks it faces in its annual report, and most of that list is last year's list. This project compared 47,643 risk sections with the same company's section from the year before, sentence by sentence. The median section keeps 78.6% of its sentences word for word, and 8.4% of it is new.

What a typical section is made of

The comparison covers 14,272,386 sentences from 10,779 companies, in reports on fiscal 2007 to 2020. Each sentence gets one label, by comparing it with every sentence in the same company's section a year earlier.

  • Kept word for word: 73.4%. Digits and capitals are ignored, so a sentence that only updates a figure or a date counts as kept.
  • Edited: 11.6%. It shares at least 19.8% of its five-word runs with one of last year's sentences.
  • Reworded: 1.6%. No shared phrasing, but a sentence-embedding model places it close to one of last year's.
  • New: 13.3%. None of the above.

Going the other way, 9.9% of last year's sentences have no match this year: they were cut.

The totals hide a wide spread. In the median filing 8.4% of the sentences are new, but a tenth of filings are under 0.5% and a tenth are over 29.2%. 4,266 filings added nothing at all.

Can the labels be trusted?

No person labeled anything in this project. The labels were checked three ways instead, and the second check failed before it passed.

Pairs whose answer is known

Real sentences were each given four partners: a light edit, a paraphrase, a version with its claim reversed, and an unrelated sentence. One language model wrote them and a second confirmed them. On the half held out for testing, a “carried over” call was right 99.8% of the time and found 96.5% of the pairs it should have.

That figure leaves the reversed claims out, and they are the method's blind spot. Of 300 sentences whose claim was reversed by changing a word or two, 292 were called carried over. The matcher follows words. So does the embedding model behind the “reworded” label: given a sentence and the same words in random order, it calls them a match 95.5% of the time.

Real pairs, two judges

How often do real filings contain such edits? 2,000 pairs of real sentences were labeled by one Claude model, and 400 of them again by a second that saw no scores. The two agreed poorly at first (a kappa of 0.34, against a bar of 0.70 set in advance), better once the instructions spelled out the cases they split on (0.60), and never well enough. By the rule fixed beforehand, the check uses only the 307 pairs both judges agree on.

The threshold that failed

The cut-off for “reworded” was first set on the constructed pairs, at a cosine of 0.774. On real pairs, 69% of what it called reworded was judged to be a different sentence. A constructed paraphrase is compared with one unrelated sentence. A real sentence is compared with every unmatched sentence in last year's filing, which is about the same business in the same vocabulary, and the closest of dozens clears a low bar easily. Set again on real pairs, the cut-off is 0.847.

What it reports when there is nothing to find

488 filings were put through the same steps against an unrelated company's previous filing in place of their own.

The same sentences, in other companies

Boilerplate also travels between companies. 7.3% of full sentences (10 words or more) are filed word for word, in the same year, by at least 5 other companies. A parent and its subsidiaries count as one company here, since their sections are near-copies of each other.

That share has grown about 6 times over: 2.2% for fiscal 2006, 9.9% for 2019, and 13.7% for 2020.

The most widely shared sentences of fiscal 2020, by rule the top of the list:

  • “Investing in our common stock involves a high degree of risk.” 246 other companies
  • “The risks and uncertainties described below are not the only ones we face.” 204 other companies
  • “As of December 31, 2020, we had an accumulated deficit of $246.0 million.” 195 other companies
  • “We may not prevail in any lawsuits that we initiate, and the damages or other remedies awarded, if any, may not be commercially meaningful.” 192 other companies
  • “Furthermore, because of the substantial amount of discovery required in connection with intellectual property litigation, there is a risk that some of our confidential information could be compromised by disclosure during this type of litigation.” 189 other companies

Digits are ignored when sentences are compared, so a sentence that states a figure matches every other company's sentence stating its own figure in the same words.

How long a sentence lasts

Following each added sentence through the same company's later filings gives it a life. That can be done for 1,884,229 sentences whose arrival was seen. The 7.1 million sentences already in a company's first filing are left out, because their age is unknown.

A new sentence has a 23% chance of being gone the next year, and the risk falls every year it survives. Half are gone within 5 filings; 30% are still there after 13.

Sentences that other companies also file last longer. A sentence filed by at least 5 other companies the year it was added has a median life of 10 filings, against 5 for a sentence of the company's own.

A model that holds fixed where a sentence sits, how long its section is, when it was added and which company wrote it says the same, and says when. A shared sentence is 42% less likely to be cut at its first filing and 30% less at its second and third. After that it is no different from any other sentence (hazard ratio 0.95, 95% interval 0.85 to 1.06). Boilerplate is protected while it is new.

Rewriting is an event, not a trait. Of the variation in how much of a filing is new, 11% belongs to the company, 4% to the year and 1% to the industry. The other 84% is the same company differing from itself from one year to the next.

Did 2020 make companies rewrite?

The pandemic gives a test. Reports on fiscal 2019 were filed across the weeks in which it began. A company that filed in early March wrote its risk section before the WHO declared a pandemic on 11 March; one that filed at the end of the month wrote it after. Late filers differ from early filers every year, so the comparison is how much that gap changed, with each company compared with itself in the three years before (9,692 filings from 4,163 companies). The design was fixed before any outcome was looked at.

They added a little, and rewrote nothing this design can detect. Late filers added sentences about COVID worth 1.3% of the section, against 0.2% for early filers. Relative to their own earlier years, their new sentences changed by +0.8 points (95% interval −0.6 to +2.3) and their removals by −0.6 (−2.1 to +0.8).

Three cautions. The contrast is diluted: 39% of the early filers had already mentioned COVID. Without comparing each company with itself, the estimate is +1.9 and its interval excludes zero (+0.6 to +3.2), which says the group of late filers changed more than their writing did: the SEC let companies file late that spring, and who did was not random. Setting exposure by the previous year's filing date, which nobody could choose in response, gives +0.4. And week by week there is no step at the cut-off.

The larger change came a year later, and it points at the SEC. Reports on fiscal 2020 have 14.8% new sentences (10.7% the year before) and cut 10.8% of the previous year's (7.9%). Only 2.5% of the section is new text that mentions COVID.

86.4% of those reports were filed after the SEC's amended risk-factor rules took effect in November 2020. The rules ask for a summary when the section is long and for generic risks to be gathered under a general heading, and a summary heading appears in 15.3% of fiscal 2020 sections, up from 0.1%. The 404 reports filed before the rules cut 6.0% of last year's sentences; the 2,563 filed after cut 11.6%. The two groups differ in other ways, so this is description and not a test.

What the first version got wrong

This is the second version of the project. A review in November 2026 retracted the first version's two headline claims, and this rework replaced them.

  • Filings are 51% alike in meaning but only 21% alike in wording. The two scores were computed on different amounts of text and can't be compared. The embedding model read about the first half page of each section.
  • A classifier sorts risks into categories with 71.2% F1. Copies of test rows were also in the training set, and the labels came from keyword rules.

The first version is kept under the v1-original tag.

Limits, and how to check this

  • No person labeled anything. Both judges are language models from one family and may share blind spots. Where they disagree, the write-up shows both.
  • An edit that changes a claim counts as carried over. A sentence kept with one word reversed is “edited”.
  • Only the previous year is compared. A sentence dropped one year and restored the next is new when it returns.
  • The 2020 test is one event, and the date a company files is not assigned at random.
