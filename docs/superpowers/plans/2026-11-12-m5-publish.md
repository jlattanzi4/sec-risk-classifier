## Global Constraints

- Every number on the page and in the README comes from `docs/data/report.json`. No number is typed into the template or the README by hand; the README's numbers are filled by the same generator (or checked against the JSON by a test).
- Examples are drawn by rule, never picked by eye. The rule is stated on the page.
- Limits are on the page, next to the claims they limit: no person labeled anything; the judges disagreed and how; the reworded tier is weak; filing date is not random.
- Charts: palette validated with dataviz's `scripts/validate_palette.js` in light and dark; a tooltip or crosshair on every chart; a table view under every chart; no dual axes; direct labels on up to four series.

- Public-facing text goes through a PR. Nothing is merged or pushed to `main`.

## Design direction (frontend-design: plan, then review against the brief)

**Subject and reader.** A study of how companies reuse the text of legal filings, read by hiring managers and senior data scientists skimming a portfolio. Its job: show that the author can measure something messy, prove the measurement works, and tell what is real from what is not.

**The idea.** Lawyers compare two versions of a document with a *redline*: unchanged text in black, deletions struck in red, insertions underlined. This project is a redline of every company's risk section against last year's, 47,643 times over. The page should look like what it measured. The current page already uses a redline for its retracted claims; the rework makes it the organizing device.

**The one bold element: a real section drawn as a redline.** The hero is one actual filing (chosen by rule: the filing closest to the median on all four shares, fiscal 2019), drawn as a strip of pages in which every sentence is a line whose length follows its length in characters, marked as the matcher labeled it: kept (ink), edited (ink with a tick), reworded, new (insertion), and last year's removed sentences struck in the margin. Hovering or focusing a line shows the sentence and the one it matched. Everything else on the page is quiet.

**Tokens** (kept from the current page, which Joe approved in sec-risk-classifier#5, with the redline colors given jobs):

| Token | Light | Job |
|---|---|---|
| paper | `#ffffff` | page |
| ink | `#1c1e21` | text, and sentences kept word for word |
| ink-2 | `#5b6068` | secondary text, axes |
| insertion | to be validated, from the current `#2a78d6` | new sentences; the page's one data hue |
| deletion | to be validated, from the current `#d03b3b` | removed sentences only; never a generic "bad" |
| edit | to be validated, an ink tint | edited and reworded: steps between kept and new |

Kept / edited / reworded / new is an ordered scale (how far a sentence moved from last year's), so the four marks are steps from ink to the insertion hue, not four unrelated colors. Removed is the only use of red. Dark mode gets its own validated steps.

**Type.** Source Serif 4 for prose and headings, Public Sans for figures, captions and tables (both already on the page). Filing excerpts are set in the serif, in the redline's own conventions (struck, underlined), not in a monospace.

**Layout.** One left-aligned reading column (36rem) with figures allowed to run to the page width (58rem). No cards, no eyebrow labels, no numbered section markers.

```
masthead
H1  How much of a risk disclosure is boilerplate?
lede: the answer in two sentences
[ hero: one real section as a redline, full width ]
1  What a typical section is made of      (stacked shares by year; spread across filings)
2  Can that be trusted?                   (tiers judged, with intervals; the threshold that failed; where it breaks)
3  The same sentences, in other companies (sharing by year; the most shared sentences)
4  How long a sentence lasts              (survival curve; what makes one last; where rewriting comes from)
5  Did 2020 make companies rewrite?       (five estimates around zero; the year after)
6  What the first version got wrong       (the two struck claims, kept short)
7  Methods, limits, reproduce
```

**Review against the brief.** Checked against the usual generated looks: not cream-and-terracotta, not near-black with an acid accent, not a broadsheet with hairline columns, not a card grid. The serif-plus-sans editorial page is common; what makes this one specific is that its marks and its hero are the redline conventions of the documents it studies, and that it uses color for exactly two things, insertion and deletion.
